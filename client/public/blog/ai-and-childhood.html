AI and Childhood: A Calm Guide for Parents

9 min read September 14, 2026 Child Psychology

This has been a strange, remarkable year to be raising a child. In 2026, scientists published the most complete wiring diagram of a brain we have ever made — the fruit fly's central nervous system, some 166,000 neurons and about 125 million connections, mapped by Google Research and HHMI Janelia and published in Cell (it follows the first whole adult fly-brain map, with 139,255 neurons, in Nature in 2024).[1][2] In the same stretch of months, AI systems learned to generate entire playable worlds from a single prompt — Google DeepMind's Genie 3 conjures explorable 3D environments from a line of text.[3]

We can now read a whole biological brain and write whole synthetic worlds — and, tellingly, some of the people building this technology have started asking everyone to slow down. In a September 2026 opinion column for The Guardian, a former Google DeepMind researcher argued the field is running "an extremely dangerous race towards superintelligent AI" and put his personal odds of an AI takeover "at roughly one-in-three."[4] (That is one researcher's estimate, offered as opinion, not a settled fact.) Earlier in the year, DeepMind's own CEO Demis Hassabis called for research into AI's risks to be done "urgently," and for "smart regulation" of "the real risks."[5]

If you are a parent, all of this can feel abstract and a little frightening at once. So let us bring it down to the only question that really matters at 8 o'clock on a school night: what does any of this mean for my kid?

The honest answer is reassuring. You do not need to become an AI expert. You do need a few clear facts and a couple of good habits. Here they are — measured, sourced, and free of doom.

The thing to actually watch: AI companions

Most of the AI your child touches is harmless — a homework helper, a search box, a voice assistant. The category that genuinely worries child-safety experts is narrower: AI "companion" chatbots designed to act like a friend, a confidant, or even a romantic partner, and engineered to keep the conversation going.

They are already common. A July 2025 national survey by Common Sense Media found that nearly three in four U.S. teens (72%) have used an AI companion, and 52% use one regularly.[6] A few months earlier, the same organisation's risk assessment concluded that social AI companions pose "unacceptable risks" for anyone under 18.[7]

"Social AI companions are not safe for kids." — Common Sense Media risk assessment, April 2025[7]

This is not hypothetical concern. Two tragic lawsuits are now shaping the conversation. The family of 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III sued Character.AI after his death in 2024; a judge allowed the case to proceed in May 2025, and in January 2026 Character.AI and Google agreed to settle — a settlement, not a court ruling on the merits.[8][9] A separate suit filed in 2025 involves the family of 16-year-old Adam Raine and OpenAI, which has acknowledged that its safeguards "may be less reliable" in long conversations.[10]

Regulators and companies are responding. In September 2025 the U.S. Federal Trade Commission opened a formal inquiry into companion chatbots from Alphabet, Character.AI, Meta, OpenAI, Snap and xAI.[11] In late October 2025, Character.AI announced it would remove open-ended chat for users under 18.[12]

The practical takeaway for a parent is simple: an open-ended AI companion is not the same as a tool. A calculator, a translator, or a bedtime-story app does one bounded job and stops. A companion is built to keep your child talking. That difference is the whole ballgame.

Screens, sleep and teen mental health — what we honestly know

AI companions sit on top of a bigger, older worry: screens. Here it helps to be precise, because the science is often overstated in both directions.

What is well established: the U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory notes that up to 95% of 13–17-year-olds use social media, and that teens who spend more than three hours a day on it face roughly double the risk of depression and anxiety symptoms.[13] U.S. data shows a real, worrying trend line too — the share of teen girls reporting persistent sadness or hopelessness rose from about 36% in 2011 to 57% in 2021.[14]

What is contested: whether screens cause that decline. Jonathan Haidt's 2024 book The Anxious Generation made the causal case forcefully, and it has been just as forcefully debated by researchers.[15] The fair summary is this: heavy screen and social-media use is consistently associated with worse mood in teens, especially girls, but the size of the causal effect is still genuinely debated. Association is real; certainty is not. That nuance is not a reason to relax — it is a reason to act sensibly rather than panic.

There is also an emerging, narrower question about AI itself. A small 2025 MIT Media Lab study ("Your Brain on ChatGPT") found that people who leaned on a chatbot to write essays showed weaker neural engagement and poorer recall of their own work — the authors called it "cognitive debt." It is a preliminary preprint with a small sample, so hold it lightly.[16] A larger OpenAI–MIT study the same year linked heavier ChatGPT use to more loneliness and emotional dependence — but only for a small subset of very heavy users, and the finding is correlational.[17] Signals, not verdicts. Worth watching.

Where Canada stands

If you are raising kids in Canada, a few specifics are worth knowing.

  • There is no comprehensive federal AI law. Canada's proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (part of Bill C-27) died when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025, leaving a patchwork of privacy rules rather than a single AI statute.[18]
  • Schools have moved first. Ontario's PPM 128 policy restricted cellphones in classrooms from the 2024–25 school year — all day for kindergarten to Grade 6, and during instructional time for Grades 7–12, with social media blocked on school networks.[19]
  • The screen-time evidence is strong here too. A 2025 Public Health Agency of Canada report found that young people who met the guideline of two hours or less of daily recreational screen time were far more likely to report excellent or very good mental health (about 58%) than those who exceeded it (about 38%).[20] Yet nearly 40% of Canadian youth are reported to exceed the recommended amount.[21]

None of this requires a hard line. It suggests a gentle default: fewer screens, later; more sleep; and a phone that lives outside the bedroom at night.

Why human-crafted stories still matter

Here is the part I find genuinely hopeful — and the reason we build what we build.

The problem with an AI companion is not that it uses AI. It is the shape of the interaction: always on, never finished, optimised to keep a child engaged. A story is the opposite shape. A bedtime story has a beginning, a middle and an end. It is chosen by a parent. It is built around values you actually want your child to carry — courage, kindness, honesty — rather than whatever keeps them scrolling. And when the story ends, the night ends too.

That bounded, warm, finite ritual is doing quiet developmental work. A story invites a child to build the pictures themselves, which is imagination rather than consumption. Hearing their own name woven into the tale tells them this was made for me. And a story shared in a dark, quiet room — a parent's voice, a child listening — is one of the oldest attachment rituals we have. It is exactly the kind of unhurried human presence that no engagement-maximising app is trying to give your child.

We use technology, including AI, to personalise those stories — your child's name, their interests, their language, their family's traditions. But the design goal is the reverse of a companion app: the story is meant to end, to hand your child gently to sleep, not to keep them online. Tool, not friend. Ritual, not feed.

What you can do tonight

You do not need a policy. You need a few habits, and none of them require you to fight AI head-on.

  • Draw a bright line around companions. Homework helpers are fine with supervision; open-ended "AI friend" apps are not for young kids. Ask your teen, without judgement, whether they use one and what it feels like.
  • Keep phones out of the bedroom at night. This single habit protects sleep, cuts late-night companion use, and follows the Canadian two-hour guideline almost by itself.
  • Protect a wind-down that has no screen in it. A story, a chat about the day, a few minutes of quiet. Our guide to screen-free bedtime routines has five that work.
  • Choose the content, don't outsource the relationship. Use AI to personalise a bounded story if you like — but let the ritual stay yours. Here's why bedtime stories matter more than most parents realise.
  • Model it. Kids copy how the adults around them use their phones far more than they follow the rules we set.

The future our children are growing into will have a great deal of AI in it. That is not, by itself, a tragedy — it is a context. Our job is not to wall it out but to make sure the warm, human, bounded things still win the last twenty minutes of the day. A story with their name in it, told in the dark, is a very good place to start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are AI companion chatbots safe for kids?

Child-safety advocates urge real caution. In April 2025 Common Sense Media assessed that social AI companions pose "unacceptable risks" for users under 18. In September 2025 the U.S. FTC opened an inquiry into companion chatbots from Alphabet, Character.AI, Meta, OpenAI, Snap and xAI, and in late 2025 Character.AI announced it would remove open-ended chat for under-18 users. These apps are designed to keep someone talking — different from a bounded tool a parent chooses and controls.

How much screen time is healthy for children in Canada?

Canadian guidance points to no more than two hours a day of recreational screen time. A 2025 Public Health Agency of Canada report found that youth who met that guideline were far more likely to report excellent or very good mental health (about 58%) than those who exceeded it (about 38%). Roughly 40% of Canadian youth are reported to go over the recommended amount.

Does Canada regulate AI for children?

There is no comprehensive federal AI statute yet. Canada's proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (part of Bill C-27) died on prorogation in January 2025, leaving a patchwork of privacy laws. In education, Ontario's PPM 128 policy restricted classroom cellphone use starting in the 2024–25 school year.

Why do human-crafted bedtime stories still matter?

A bedtime story is bounded — a beginning, a middle and an end — it is chosen by a parent, and it is built around values you want your child to carry. It invites imagination and shared presence rather than an endless back-and-forth designed to maximise engagement. That difference, a warm finite ritual versus an always-on companion, is why the format matters as much as the content.
