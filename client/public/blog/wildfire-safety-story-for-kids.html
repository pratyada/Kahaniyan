A Bedtime Story About Wildfires — Helping Kids Feel Calm & Prepared

6 min read July 16, 2026 New Story

When the sky turns orange and the air smells like smoke, grown-ups reach for the news and the air-quality app. But what about the little ones? Kids notice everything — the haze, the closed windows, the cancelled park trip — and when they don't understand something, they fill the gap with worry.

So Dr. Sleepy made one. In "When the Sky Turns Orange" — Episode 6 of Dr. Sleepy's Parenting Corner — a gentle doctor answers a parent's real questions about smoky skies, wildfires burning across Canada, and how to keep little lungs safe. Because the best way to help a child feel calm isn't to hide the smoke — it's to help them understand it.

Why a wildfire story helps

Children are wonderfully literal. A wildfire far away can feel, to a five-year-old, like it's right outside the door. A calm, well-told story does three quiet things at once:

  • It names the scary thing. Smoke, haze, "the air is resting today" — giving it words shrinks it down to something a child can hold.
  • It shows a plan. In the story, a family closes the windows, turns on the little air cleaner, and reads together inside. Kids learn that grown-ups have a plan — and that feels safe.
  • It ends cozy. Every My Sleepy Tale story lands somewhere warm and sleepy. The worry goes out with the light.

Understanding something is what makes it less scary. That's true for wildfires, thunderstorms, and just about everything else a small heart worries about at bedtime.

What kids learn (without it feeling like a lesson)

  • Why the sky sometimes looks orange, and that it passes.
  • That closing windows and running an air cleaner keeps indoor air fresh.
  • Why we take it easy outside on smoky days — and that it's just for a little while.
  • That firefighters and grown-ups all over the world are helping.

Then let them explore the real thing — safely

Here's the part kids love. After the story, curious little explorers can play with a live 3D globe — a real, interactive planet they can spin with their finger to see worldwide wildfires and the air-quality (AQI) index in real time. Where are fires burning right now? Which countries have the smokiest air today? It's all there, live from NASA and global sensors — turning a scary, invisible thing into something a child can look at, point to, and understand. (Grown-up supervision recommended — it's real data, and it's fascinating.)
